# Overview

A diagnostic research pathway, not a single deployment: interview
insights from ~40 conversations with organizations, investors,
startups, and Voice AI providers (led by Manmath Goel, EkStep
Foundation Voice AI team fellow). The research question changed after
the first observations — it did not begin as an organizational-
readiness study. It started narrower: why were organizations not
adopting Voice AI despite significant financial support (EkStep had
already subsidised access)? Only a small number of subsidised
organizations actively experimented, and fewer still progressed to
pilots.

**Note on source material.** This source is a cross-organization
research summary, not a stage-by-stage account of one deployment. It
doesn't carry "deployment at a glance" scale figures or an
Explore/Define/Pilot/Scale annexure the way the other pathways in this
library do — most of its content sits at what this library calls the
Explore stage (diagnosing why adoption stalls before it starts), and
many stage cells below are honestly thin or not documented for that
reason.

# Problem

## Explore

The constraint is organisational absorption capacity, not technology or
price. Technology itself was rarely the immediate reason deployments
stalled — many organizations already had technical teams and access to
capable vendors. What they lacked was confidence and clarity about how
to introduce a new technology into an existing organisation. Unanswered
questions came before model quality: is our problem actually suitable
for AI? What does success look like? Who owns the deployment? How do we
work with the vendor? What happens after the pilot?

# Technology

## Explore

Deliberately the negative-space finding of this research: technology
readiness and organizational readiness are different capabilities, and
adoption depends far more on the latter than organizations typically
anticipate. Model quality and infrastructure become relevant only after
the organisational questions are answered.

## Pilot

One concrete operational tactic, from a Karnataka government
agriculture outbound-calling use case: calling farmers between 7-8am
(when they indicated they were most likely to answer), sending a prior
WhatsApp/text alert before the call, and triggering two or three call
attempts at intervals — together moved pickup rates from 30-35% to
60-70%, without any change to the underlying model.

# Institution

## Explore

Organisation readiness breaks into six discrete workstreams: use-case
definition (what problem, what success looks like), vendor selection
(evaluated against the specific use case), conversation design
(scripting, decision branches, handling background noise), QA audit
(call-by-call verification the bot captures/routes information
correctly), change management (preparing the organisation and its
users), and workflow/operating-model integration (folding the tool into
how the organisation already runs). No single organization exhibited
all six gaps at once — each showed some combination, shaped by its own
context.

## Define

AI implementation is primarily a management challenge, not a software
purchase. Organisations that progressed successfully invested time in
coordinating internal teams, defining responsibilities, managing vendor
relationships, reviewing outputs, and making continuous adjustments —
resembling project management more than software deployment.

## Pilot

QA discipline determines whether pilots improve or stagnate. Many
pilots produced inaccurate or unusable data not because the technology
failed, but because there was no structured process for reviewing
calls, identifying errors, and feeding improvements back.

# Ecosystem

## Explore

The ecosystem needs implementation guidance, not just better
technology. Organizations lacked implementation checklists, evaluation
frameworks, practical questions to ask vendors, and examples of
successful implementation processes — the missing resource was
practical implementation knowledge, not another AI platform.

## Define

Implementation knowledge is highly transferable even when use cases
differ — agriculture, education, civic engagement, and employment
platforms all struggled with ownership, testing, vendor collaboration,
and workflow integration. Organizations don't necessarily need examples
from their own sector; they need implementation knowledge that
transfers across sectors.

# Workforce

## Explore

Adoption requires behavioural change as much as technological change.
Organizations hesitate not because they distrust the technology, but
because they're uncertain whether the effort required to adopt it will
generate sufficient value — the same uncertainty that delays individual
adoption of a new tool, at organizational scale.

# Operating Model

## Explore

Not distinctly documented separately in this source.

## Define

Assign a single accountable owner to lead the deployment end-to-end.
Use a PMU-style approach with regular vendor collaboration and rapid
feedback loops.

## Pilot

Organizations that struggled treated pilots as demonstrations.
Organizations that succeeded treated pilots as learning systems,
continuously asking: what failed? why? what should be adjusted? how
quickly can we test another version? The speed of learning became a
competitive advantage.

# Lessons for the Next Adopter

* Implementation research should continually challenge its initial assumptions — the most important findings often emerge after discovering the original hypothesis is wrong.
* Technology readiness and organizational readiness are different capabilities; treat the latter as most of the effort, not a formality.
* Break implementation into explicit workstreams (use case, vendor, conversation design, QA, change management, workflow integration) with defined ownership.
* Seek implementation knowledge across sectors, not only within your own — the patterns transfer more than the specific solutions do.
* Assign a single accountable owner end-to-end, and treat pilots as learning systems, not demonstrations.
